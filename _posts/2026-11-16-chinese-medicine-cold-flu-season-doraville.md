## How Chinese medicine looks at a cold

In TCM, a common cold is often described as an outside "pathogen" getting past the body's surface. The surface is guarded by what TCM calls **Wei Qi**, sometimes translated as "defensive Qi." When Wei Qi is strong, the idea goes, you shrug off the bug your coworker has. When you're run down, short on sleep or chilled, it gets in more easily.

TCM also sorts colds by how they show up, because different patterns call for different care:

- **Wind-Cold:** chills that feel worse than the fever, body aches, a stiff neck, a runny nose with clear mucus, and no thirst. This is the classic "I got caught in the cold rain" cold.
- **Wind-Heat:** more fever than chills, a sore or red throat, yellow mucus, thirst and a headache.
- **Damp patterns:** a heavy, foggy feeling, upset stomach or loose stools along with cold symptoms.

That's why two people with "a cold" may get very different herbs from Dr. Xu.

## Why the first sniffle matters

Chinese medicine puts a lot of weight on catching a cold early, while it's still at the surface. In practice, that means paying attention in the first day or two, when you notice the first tickle in your throat, the first chill, or that achy, "something's coming on" feeling.

That early stretch is usually the best time to call us. In TCM thinking, once a cold has settled in deeper, with a cough that hangs on for weeks, it usually takes longer to work through.

## Simple habits Chinese medicine recommends

Our family's advice for cold and flu season is mostly common sense, and much of it lines up with what your doctor would say:

- **Keep your neck and back covered.** TCM sees the back of the neck as a place where wind and cold get in. A scarf on a chilly morning at the MARTA station is old wisdom.
- **Rest early.** Going to bed when you first feel off is better than pushing through.
- **Eat warm, simple food.** Soups, congee and cooked vegetables are easier on digestion than cold or greasy food when you're sick.
- **Warm drinks.** Many families brew a simple ginger and scallion tea at the first chill. It's a kitchen tradition, not a prescription, so check with Dr. Xu before using it if you take medication or have a health condition.
- **Wash your hands and stay home when you're sick.** No herb replaces that.

If your nose stays stuffy long after the cold is gone, it may be allergies or sinus trouble instead. Read our post on [acupuncture for allergies and sinus trouble](/blog/acupuncture-allergies-sinus-doraville).

## When to see your doctor or urgent care instead

Please don't wait on Chinese medicine if you have any of these. See your doctor, go to urgent care, or call 911:

- Trouble breathing, shortness of breath or chest pain
- A high fever, or a fever that lasts more than a few days
- Confusion, a severe headache with a stiff neck, or fainting
- Signs of dehydration, such as very little urine
- Symptoms that get better and then suddenly get worse
- A sick infant, or any concern if you're pregnant, over 65, or have asthma, diabetes, heart disease or a weakened immune system

Flu can be serious, and your doctor can test for it and, in some cases, prescribe antiviral medicine that works best when started early.

## FAQ

### Can Chinese medicine cure a cold or the flu?

No treatment cures the common cold, and we don't promise that herbs will. Chinese medicine aims to support your body and ease symptoms based on your pattern. Many patients like having it as part of their care, alongside their doctor's advice.

### Should I still get a flu shot?

That's a question for your doctor, and we encourage you to follow their advice. Chinese medicine is meant to work alongside regular medical care, not replace it.

### Can I take Chinese herbs with cold medicine?

Sometimes, but tell Dr. Xu about every medicine and supplement you take, including over-the-counter cold and flu products. Some don't mix well with certain herbs.

### Are cold and flu herbs safe for children?

Children need different care and doses. Call us first, and always involve your child's pediatrician.
